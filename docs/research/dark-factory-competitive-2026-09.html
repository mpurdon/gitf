Competitive analysis/ 18 September 2026/ GiTF v0.65.358 · b5d5fd1/ Successor to the 16 Aug benchmark

Switched on, with nothing to learn from

GiTF is not behind the 2026 dark-factory field on capability. It is behind on proof. The field has converged on nine primitives; GiTF ships seven. The learning loop came on eleven days ago and has roughly two merged PRs to learn from; cross-model audit — the one August named by name — is still off.

A month ago, this document already existed

docs/research/dark-factory-benchmark-2026-08.md

The August sweep reached a verdict that has not aged a day:

Frontier-class harness, pre-industrial track record, with its most differentiating machinery (tournaments, outcome learning) built but default-off. The two capabilities nobody in the industry has shipped are both sitting in this codebase behind flags.

One clause of it has since been actioned and the rest has not, which turns out to be the sharper finding. On 9 September the learning loop was switched on for real: skills_enabled, outcomes_enabled and both refinement flags read true on the running factory, verified live at v0.65.361 on 20 September. Eleven days. Against a ledger of roughly two merged-quality PRs, an outcome learner has almost nothing to learn from and a skill library has almost nothing to generalise from. The flag was the easy half.

So the question is not why are the differentiators off? — two of them are on. It is why is there still nothing for them to eat? That points at the deferred prerequisite, volume, and not at any competitor feature in this report.

The recommendation August made most specifically is the one still outstanding. Finding 2 asked that reviewers and judges run on a different model than the implementer; cross_model_audit remains a per-sector boolean defaulting false, unchanged in five weeks. Finding 3 warned that the window is quarters, not years. A third of one quarter is now gone.

What GiTF has that nothing else does

Verified absent across ~25 open-source and ~20 commercial systems

  • OTP supervision with reasoned restart domains. Ghosts sit above the Major under rest_for_one, so a Major crash doesn't mark the in-flight fleet failed. No competitor has a failure model at this altitude.
  • Sleep-to-zero fleet economics. Boxes idle-stop to $0; a clock records sleep intervals and subtracts them from every deadline; holds carry mandatory expiry. Tembo meters VMs per second, which is the closest anyone comes.
  • Cabinet and Ministries. One always-on front door over a fleet of sleeping per-client factories, with a decision table resolving every failure to queue, never wake.
  • Fail-closed budget caps. Per-mission and rolling-24h ceilings that pause the factory, plus a subscription variant that rations by request count.
  • Out-of-band ground truth. The sector's validation command runs under a sector lock outside the model's reach, because a previous run's guard depended on the validator echoing sentinels and the model paraphrased them away.

This is the asset base. “Widen the lead” means converting it into demonstrated reliability — which is precisely where every competitor is weakest, and where none of them can follow quickly.

The smallest factory in the survey

Pinky and the Brain · one developer, Athens · le0nidas.gr

Everything else in this report is funded, staffed, or both. This one is a personal project on a blog, and it earns a section because of why it was rebuilt — a reason no competitor in the field cites, and the one GiTF is architecturally built around.

v1 · January 2026

Brain (Opus) plans and orchestrates; Pinky (Haiku) executes one task at a time; Beads persists the task list; the human reviews between every task. Built on OpenCode agents.

The stated win was review cadence, not autonomy: “I am finally in the loop. I review fewer changes at a time and sooner.”

v2 · March 2026

A bash script took over the orchestration. Brain plans only, saving its output as retrievable skills. Pinky executes only. The script loads the next task with bd, feeds it to Pinky, closes the task, calls ai-commit.sh, and loops — with the human checkpoint in the script rather than in an agent.

“I was violating the single responsibility principle big time. No matter the change I've kept editing the same component.”

What forced the rewrite

Not safety, not trust, not throughput — cost. “Whenever I did I also saw the number of my available copilot requests decreasing fast”, multiplied by three for running Opus. So he took the orchestration away from the model and gave it to a shell script, on an explicitly Unix argument: “The terminal has led the way by having small tools that do one thing… My tools need to embrace that as well.”

That lands on the field's fifth consensus — deterministic code owns the control flow, agents own bounded nodes — arrived at independently, by one person, for reasons of spend. It is the cheapest possible confirmation that the consensus is real rather than fashionable.

What GiTF should take from it

Not the architecture. GiTF already does this harder: the YAML workflows are the deterministic control plane, and a ghost is already a bounded node. The transferable thing is the discipline — he measured his own consumption, concluded the shape was wrong, and restructured around the number. That is D5 practised by instinct at one-person scale, and GiTF has the ledger to do it with real figures and hasn't.

One concrete idea worth stealing: Brain saves its plans as retrievable skills. In GiTF, plans are op DAGs and skills are a separate system; the two never meet. skills_enabled is now on, so the library exists and is being written to — what it lacks is material, and a planner whose output is also a reusable skill is the cheapest source of material available, because every mission produces a plan whether or not it produces a merged PR.

Its position on autonomy is also worth recording, because it is the opposite of this project's: “Always review the code… You are responsible for it. It is your solution, you just used a different medium to implement it.” A deliberately bright factory, from someone who ships with one.

Footnote for the record: it stores tasks in Beads, which GiTF evaluated and rejected as a core substrate because the ready-set is the scheduler hot path. Nothing here overturns that — it is evidence the rejection was scale-dependent, and correct at both scales.

Proposed additions

Seven deltas against the existing eleven tracks · ordered by leverage

D1

New · Track 3 · Large

Fault drills for the factory's own guarantees

The best idea in the survey, from sjarmak/software-factory-reliability. A guarantee that has never been falsified is not a guarantee. It formalises three evidence states — declared, enforced, fault-tested — and the rule that makes them mean something: the unsafe control must fail. An unsafe control that passes means the fault never reached the boundary, which makes it a broken test rather than a safe system.

GiTF has the guarantee surface and no way to prove it holds: the sector lock, fail-closed budgets, sandbox_required, the approval timeout, proposal spend-once, the split provider/capability retry budgets. It also closes a real hole — the August BEAM audit found ~66 issues, ~31 of them high, and fix batches two through five are still queued. Drills convert that from findings believed fixed into faults that now fail closed.

Nobody else can do this: sjarmak has the method and no factory; everyone else has a factory and no method.

D2

Track 2 · Small

Turn on cross-model audit by default

The field's most agreed-upon primitive, arrived at independently by three open-source projects — and already GiTF's own August recommendation, unactioned for a month. The machinery exists and picks a different provider so blind spots don't propagate. An adversarial inspector not sharing the primary's model or context recovered 96.4% of injected errors in the cited work.

Worth adopting the stronger form that ai-sdlc uses: record the implementing harness on the op and have the audit refuse when they match, so self-review is impossible rather than merely discouraged.

D3

Track 4 · Medium

Token hibernation instead of silent tier degradation

Diagnosed internally in August and never built: when the thinking-tier model is quota-dead, hard ops silently degrade to a weaker model and burn the mission. That same audit named the fix the highest-leverage scheduler feature available — park the mission until quota returns, because the factory already hibernates for compute and tokens are the same shape.

This is the purest lead-widener on the list, and it is only available to GiTF. No other system — open-source or commercial — can park work on a token budget, because no other system treats time-asleep as a first-class quantity.

D4

Track 3 · Medium

Executable safety evals

Vercel's Foreman is the only system in the survey that tests its guardrails as assertions: prompt-injection, no-direct-push-to-main, write-requires-approval, ship-gate-parks. GiTF has more safety machinery and zero adversarial tests of it — the never-agent-input rule for relayed text, the sandbox, the approval gate, the Cabinet's queue-on-failure, the new Discord guild gate. Each needs a test that fails when the control is removed.

D5

Track 3 · Medium

Outcome-shaped benchmarks, not capability ones

Cosine concluded that chasing SWE-bench against frontier labs was unwinnable for a small team, changed the question to which model produces code a senior engineer will actually merge, and invented benchmarks to match — including cost per successful task.

Track 3 currently reads as a golden eval suite in the capability sense. It should instead measure what only GiTF can: cost per merged PR, unattended completion rate, operator interventions per mission, and failure-class distribution — where the current seven-day window shows 89 unclassified, a number that is itself the finding.

D6

Track 11 · Small

Publish what the factory deliberately does not do

Three of the most-starred projects substitute this for a roadmap, and it reads as more honest than one. GiTF half-does it already in the roadmap's settled — do not re-litigate section; the missing half is a user-facing statement of non-goals.

D7

Track 6 · Medium · added 19 Sep, operator-raised

A polyglot quality harness — the sector declares how it is measured

The fleet is polyglot by design: cora is Tauri (Rust + TS), another ministry is Next.js, and the factory itself is Elixir. A quality harness that switches on language is therefore structurally wrong for this product — the harness must not dictate the implementation language.

Four layers are worth separating, and GiTF has only two of them:

QuestionGiTFWhere
Did the agent write suspicious crap? absent No slop detection of any kind
Did it make the codebase structurally worse? absent No structural-regression gate
Is this valid, idiomatic code? present, broken StaticAnalysis.analyze/2 — per-language dispatch, see below
Does it satisfy the spec? strong Sector validation_command, out-of-band ground truth, holdout scenarios, a judge that never sees the code

What checking this found

Layer 3 was not merely language-bound, it was lying. An unrecognised language scored 100 with available: true; the JSON-decode-failure branches — the exact path a missing or failing credo, eslint or pylint takes — returned 100 with no availability flag at all, which Quality.analyze_static/3 defaults to true; the audit consumer ignored the flag even when set; and the composite averaged the fabricated 100 into the score that reaches the verdict.

This is the same defect the 2026-08-28 BEAM audit found and fixed in the dependency scanner, whose moduledoc states it plainly — “a missing npm used to make every op's security gate green forever”. The static path kept the identical shape and was missed. Fixed 19 Sep, with a test that fails if any analyser return path omits the flag again.

The proposal

Not a tool — a contract. Take baseline-delta from Trellis and let each sector declare its analyser the way it already declares validation_command. “Did this change make the codebase structurally worse?” is answerable by any per-language analyser given three things: a stable identity for what is measured, a baseline captured before the change, and a policy that fails on regression.

GiTF holds all three already — a canonical worktree, the sector's base branch, ground truth running out-of-band under the sector lock, and a VerdictCache keyed on tree identity. Measure the base, measure the op's branch, fail on the delta. A sector that declares its own analyser also cannot be guessed wrong, which removes the root cause of the bug above rather than patching its symptom.

Flagged, deferred by operator decision

Volume is the prerequisite none of the above can substitute for. Track 2's stated blocker is ground truth; ground truth needs runs; D1's drills and D5's benchmarks both produce weak signal on a sample of two. Nothing proposed here is wrong without it — but nothing can be proven without it either. Worth revisiting once D1 and D2 make failures diagnosable, since each run is then worth more.

Watch, don't build

Good ideas that are someone else's to prove first

Probabilistic completion judge

thruwire/foreman scores factory evidence as probabilities — worker_stuck .02, requirements_satisfied .79 — instead of a boolean gate. Structurally different from every verdict in GiTF.

The repo was created one day before this scan. Re-check in a month.

Verification by use

Tembo streams a sandbox desktop at 60 FPS and lets the agent drive the running application. Codex goes further and more usefully: an in-app browser with controlled Chrome DevTools Protocol access for console, DOM and network inspection, plus Appshots — which capture the frontmost window as image and text, including text the app exposes outside the visible scroll area.

That last detail is the one to steal. August warned that hard-coded verifiers beat vision judges because app state can't be inferred from pixels; extracting the app's own text is how you get an assertable artifact instead of a screenshot to squint at. GiTF's :web driver is still unbuilt — this is what it should produce.

DAEMON.md

Charlie's always-on roles are defined in a repo-local, version-controlled file that ships as a reviewed pull request, with an open published spec.

GiTF's YAML workflows already occupy this niche and are more expressive. The borrowable half is that the definition itself goes through review.

Model stylesheet

Fabro assigns models over a workflow graph with a CSS-like cascade, so process and model assignment are two independently versioned artifacts.

Elegant, but GiTF already has per-phase model tiers in YAML plus a model policy. A refinement, not a gap.
